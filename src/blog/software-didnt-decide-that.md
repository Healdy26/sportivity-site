There is a sentence that is going to get said in a lot of small businesses this winter, and it will come out a bit too quickly.

"We'll not bother replacing her. The system can pick that up now."

Nobody will argue. It sounds modern and sensible, and it saves a salary. And three months later, I would put money on it, somebody is stood in the office asking why the big customer has gone quiet, and nobody can quite work out when it started.

## Icing and cake

On 22 October Barclays [announced it is cutting](https://www.itv.com/news/2026-10-22/barclays-to-cut-5000-jobs-this-year-as-ai-makes-work-more-efficient) around 5,000 jobs by the end of the year, about five per cent of its people. The framing from the finance director was that AI is making their people more productive. About 60 per cent of the cuts land in support functions, and that includes software developers.

In the same results, the chief executive, C.S. Venkatakrishnan, said this: "We will employ AI at scale. But AI is the icing on the cake, not the cake."

I want to be fair here. I have no idea what the right size for Barclays is, and neither does anybody shouting about it on my feed. Sometimes a business needs to get smaller. He was also clear that most of the savings so far came from years of investing in technology and simplifying how they work, not from a chatbot. That is an honest answer.

But read the two lines next to each other. If AI is only the icing, then the cake is a decision people made, in a room, about people. The tool gets the headline. The choice belongs to a human.

And that matters a lot more to you than it does to a bank.

## Why it lands harder at thirty people

A big bank has cushion. If it gets a call slightly wrong, the damage gets spread across a balance sheet and a financial year.

You do not have that. In a firm of 30, every person is carrying something that is not in their job description. Usually more than one thing. The office manager who is the only one who knows which supplier needs ringing on a Thursday. The lad on the tools who keeps your best customer sweet because he gets on with their foreman.

None of that is written down. So when you decide AI can "pick that bit up", you are deciding on the bit you can see. The bit you cannot see walks out of the door with them.

You feel a bad call like that inside a month, not inside a financial year.

## Say it in your own name

Here is my stance, and I will not dress it up. If you decide to run leaner, say so. Out loud, in your own name.

"I am choosing to run with one fewer person, and here is what we are going to stop doing."

People can take that. They might not like it, but they can deal with an adult telling them the truth. What they cannot deal with is being told a piece of software made the decision on its own. They know it did not. And from then on, every time you bring in a new tool, they will hear a threat.

I am not anti-AI. I use it every single day, it is basically my PA. My view has not changed: I don't think AI is going to replace humans, but it will replace certain roles and jobs. Which is exactly why the decision about which roles has to be made by somebody with a name, who can be asked about it afterwards.

## The fortnight before you decide

So before you decide not to replace somebody, or to hand part of a job to a tool, do this. It costs you nothing but a bit of attention.

For two weeks, ask the person to keep a plain list of what they actually do. Not their job description. What they did, in their own words, a line each, as it happens. The phone call. The chasing. The little fix nobody asked for.

Then sit down with the list, together, and sort every line into three piles:

- The things a tool can genuinely do today.
- The things a tool could help with, but a person still needs to own.
- The things that only happen because of who that person is.

I would bet on that third pile surprising you. There is nearly always a job in there that nobody knew existed until it was written down.

Then make your decision. You might still go leaner, and that is fine. But you will be making it with your eyes open, and you will be able to say why.

## Who is carrying the can

Every business is going to make calls like this over the next few years. Some of them will be right. The ones that go wrong will mostly go wrong in the same way. A person decided, and a tool got the blame.
