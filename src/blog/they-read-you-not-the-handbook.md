There is a drawer in most kitchens with the instructions in it. The boiler, the oven, the smoke alarm. Nobody has opened it since the week you moved in. You learned how the house works by living in it.

Your staff handbook is in that drawer.

You wrote it properly. Or you paid somebody to, and you read every page. There is a line in it about wellbeing, something like "we encourage everyone to switch off outside working hours". You meant it when you signed it off. I'd bet you still do.

Then on a Thursday night at ten to ten you remembered something about Friday's delivery, and you sent a quick message to get it out of your head. One of your team answered at six minutes past.

Nobody broke a rule. But she knows how the house works now, and it is not what the drawer says.

## More than half

Zara Whysall and her colleagues at Nottingham Business School asked more than 1,170 people working in UK organisations whether what their employer writes down matches what they see day to day. [On wellbeing, 53% said it does not](https://www.ntu.ac.uk/about-us/news/news-articles/2026/10/organisational-culture-gaps-leadership). It was the biggest gap they found.

They gave it a name that sticks. HR whitewashing. The firm makes the commitment, and the people inside it never see anybody behave differently.

The study looked at medium and large organisations. Don't let that comfort you. In a firm of twelve there is no HR department stood between you and the policy. You are the policy. If it does not match, there is only one person it does not match with.

## Writing it down feels like doing it

I don't believe anybody writes a wellbeing policy in bad faith. People don't do things wrong on purpose.

Here is how good owners end up here anyway. Getting a policy written is real work. It takes evenings. So when it is finished, it feels like the job is finished. The document becomes the achievement.

I build quality systems for a living, and I work off a simple chain. Policy drives process, and process is people doing things together. A policy that never changes what somebody does on a wet Tuesday is a wish.

Your team don't judge you on the page. They judge you on what you do when the page is inconvenient. Who gets asked to stay late. What happens to the person who takes the full fortnight off. Whether you answer emails from the beach.

## What it really costs you

The wellbeing gap is the cheap part.

Every time what you write and what you do come apart, your people learn a rule. What the boss writes down is the nice version. And they don't keep that rule in the wellbeing folder. They use it on the lot. The values on the wall. The plan you presented in January. The email that says nobody's job is at risk.

That matters to you more than most, because you want out of the day to day. The only way out is for people to run things off what is written down, without you stood there. If your written word carries no weight, nothing counts until you have said it in person. So you say it in person. Again. And you wonder why you cannot take a week off.

Can you see what that is? A business that only works out loud.

## Three lines and two people

Do this before Christmas. It takes an hour.

Take three lines out of your handbook or your values, the ones you are proudest of. Under each one, write what a person would see you do if it were true. Something you could film. "We value wellbeing" becomes "I don't message after six, and if I think of something, I schedule it for the morning."

If you cannot write the behaviour, the line is decoration. Decide what it means or cut it.

Then ask two people in the business one question. "When did you last see this happen?" And be quiet. If they have to think for a long while, you have your answer, and you got it cheaply.

Some lines will turn out not to be true, and you will know you are not going to make them true. Take those out. A thin handbook people believe is worth more than a thick one in the drawer.
