TITLE: The Most Expensive Two Days in Your Career
SUBTITLE: I lost a piece of work I was sure I had. The email did not do the damage. The next two days did, and the thing I quietly decided about myself in them. Here is the rule I use now.

I lost a piece of work last year that I had genuinely expected to get.

Not hoped. Expected. I had done the thinking, I liked the people, and I had built the whole thing in my head already, down to how I would run the first session. Then the email came. It was perfectly kind, and it said they had gone another way.

I was fine for about an hour. Properly fine. Very adult about it.

Then I was not fine.

And here is the bit I have only really worked out in the last couple of years. The email did not do the damage. The email was one sentence about one decision, made by people with their own reasons that had very little to do with me. What did the damage was the next two days, and the thing I quietly decided about myself in them.

## What happens in the forty eight hours

You do not sit down and decide you are not good enough. Nobody does that on purpose.

What happens is smaller and much sneakier than that. You go back over it. You find the bit you were not sure about, the answer you fumbled, the slide you rushed. And you build a line out of it. Something like: I am not quite at that level yet. Or: I am alright with the ones who already know me, I just do not win the new ones.

Then you carry on as normal. That is the clever bit. Nothing looks different from the outside, and nobody would ever guess. But the line is in now, and it starts choosing things for you. You put yourself up for one fewer thing. You do not ring the person you were going to ring. Six months later you are aiming lower and you are not aware of having made a single decision about it.

I wrote last week about Chris Argyris and the ladder of inference, and how a manager can build a permanent verdict on somebody out of one blush in a meeting three years earlier. Same machinery, exactly. The only difference is that this time you are doing it to yourself, and there is nobody else in the room to push back and ask you what made you say that.

## The rule I use now

So I have a rule, and it is not a nice motivational one. It is just a rule.

I am not allowed to decide anything about myself for forty eight hours.

Feel it, yes. Be annoyed. Be flat. Go and sit by the water for a bit and sulk about it, because pretending you are not bothered is its own kind of damage. But no verdicts. Not for two days. The sting tells you how much you wanted it. It tells you nothing at all about whether you are any good.

Then on the third day I do two things.

First, I write down what actually happened, in facts only. Not what it meant. Just: I was one of four, they went with somebody else, I do not know why. Reading that back is almost boring, and that is the whole point. Boring is the correct size for it.

Second, I go and get one real piece of information. I ring them. Not to argue, and not fishing for a bit of reassurance. One question: what would I have needed to be the obvious choice? Most people will answer that, because it costs them nothing and you have made it easy. And the answer is almost never the thing I had been chewing on for two days. It is usually something flat and fixable. They wanted somebody who had already done it in a school. The budget moved. The decision was made before I ever walked in.

That is the difference between a fact and a verdict. A fact gives you something to do on Monday. A verdict just sits there and quietly runs your year.

## If this is your second or third no

If you are employed, ambitious, and you have now been passed over more than once, go back and read that last bit again, because it matters a good deal more for you than it does for me.

A survey of 2,000 UK workers for Ciphr earlier this year found 22% wanted to leave over a lack of progression, and 24% over feeling undervalued. Big numbers. And I would bet a fair few of those people never once asked the direct question. They got the no, they wrote the private verdict, and then they went and did the same job somewhere else with the same story about themselves still in their pocket.

So ask. One uncomfortable phone call, set against a year of quietly aiming lower.

And if you are the one giving the no, you have a job here too. "It was really close" is not feedback. It is a kindness that costs somebody twelve months. Tell them the actual thing.

## Nobody gets knocked back once

You will get a few of these. They will all sting, and the sting is not the problem.

The problem is that you are the only person in the building writing your file, and you tend to sit down and write it on the worst day.

So have a proper think about this one rather than scrolling straight past it. What did you decide about yourself the last time something did not go your way? And would you still say it out loud now?
