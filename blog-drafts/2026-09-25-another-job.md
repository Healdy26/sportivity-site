An owner I know rang me in a bit of a state a few weeks back. He'd bought the licences. Forty odd seats, the good tier, the one that costs proper money. He'd done the all staff email. He'd even got someone in to run a lunchtime session with sandwiches.

Four months on, near enough nothing had changed. Two people were using it properly. Everyone else had opened it once, had a poke about, and gone back to doing the job the way they did it in March.

"I don't get it," he said. "I've given them the tool."

And that's the bit. He had given them the tool. He'd given them absolutely nothing else.

## Gallup put a number on it

I don't usually lead with research, but this one stopped me.

Gallup looked at what actually separates the organisations where AI has changed how work gets done from the ones where it hasn't. Not the tools. Not the budget. Not the sector.

The manager.

Where people strongly agree that their manager champions this stuff, 33 per cent say AI has transformed how work gets done. Where they don't, it's 4 per cent.

Same software. Same rollout. Same sandwiches. Eight times the difference, and the only variable in the middle is one person.

There's a second number in there too, and it's the one that got my back up. Half of HR directors say they're not confident their managers can guide their people through this.

Half. Not confident.

Now, I've sat in enough of those conversations to know what that sentence really means when it's said out loud in a boardroom. It means: our managers are the problem.

I think that's the wrong diagnosis, and I think it's a convenient one.

## What you actually asked them to do

Picture the middle manager in that owner's business the morning the all staff email landed.

She has a team of nine. Two vacancies she's been carrying since spring. A budget that got trimmed in the last round. A monthly report that takes her the best part of two days. Appraisals due. Someone off with stress and a return to work to handle gently.

And now, in among all that, a new expectation: lead your team through a fundamental change in how they do their work. Learn a technology nobody has taught you. Work out which bits of your team's job it should touch and which bits it absolutely shouldn't. Handle the person who's quietly terrified it's coming for their role. Do all of that visibly and confidently, in front of nine people who will read your face before they read the guidance.

What came off her plate to make room? Nothing. What did she get in the way of time, cover, or a straight answer on what good looks like? Nothing.

Then we call her a blocker.

She's not blocking anything. She's the only person in the building who's been handed the change and the day job at the same time and asked to do both well.

## Championing isn't a personality trait

Here's what I've come to believe after watching a fair few of these rollouts land, and some of them land badly.

"Champion" gets talked about like it's a type of person. The enthusiastic one. The early adopter. The one who's good with tech. Find those, the thinking goes, and get them to spread it about.

It isn't a type of person. It's a set of conditions.

Someone champions a change when three things are true. They've had enough time with it to be genuinely useful rather than just positive about it. They know exactly what their leader expects and by when. And they have permission to be visibly rubbish at it for a fortnight while they learn.

That last one does more damage than the other two put together, and it's the one nobody talks about.

A manager's authority in front of their team is built on being the person who knows. Ask them to publicly fumble with something new, in front of the people they're meant to be leading, with no explicit cover from above, and most will quietly decide the safest move is to not bother. Not out of laziness. Out of a completely rational read of the room.

So they hedge. They say the right things in the leadership meeting and change nothing on Monday. And four months later the owner rings someone like me, baffled.

## The honest test

If your rollout has stalled, here's the question I'd put to you, and I'd want a specific answer rather than a general feeling.

Name the last thing you took off a manager's plate when you added this to it.

Not "we gave them training." Not "we told them it was a priority." What did you remove? Which report did you kill, which meeting did you cancel, which deadline did you move, to create the hours this actually needs?

If you can't name one, you haven't asked them to lead a change. You've asked them to absorb one. Those are very different requests and only one of them works.

And while you're at it, ask yourself whether you've ever said out loud, to their faces, that it's fine to be clumsy with this in front of their team for a few weeks. Because if you haven't said it, they've assumed the opposite. People always do.

## The culture is the product

I wrote a while back about [briefing AI like you'd brief a good new starter](/blog/new-starter-in-your-pocket/), and I stand by every word of it. But that piece was about the individual skill. This one's about the thing sat underneath it, and it's bigger.

Buying the tool is the easy bit. It's a purchase order and an afternoon. What you're actually trying to change is how a group of people work together, what they think is safe to try, and what they believe will happen to them if they get it wrong.

That's culture. It always was. Nobody has ever bought their way to it.

Which is why the same rollout, the same licences and the same lunchtime session produce a third of people saying everything's changed in one team and four per cent in the team next door. The variable isn't the software. It's whether someone in the middle was actually set up to lead it, or just handed it.

So my question this week is a blunt one, and it isn't really about AI.

The last big change you rolled out. Did you give your managers a job, or did you just give them another one?

Have a think about that before you buy anything else.
