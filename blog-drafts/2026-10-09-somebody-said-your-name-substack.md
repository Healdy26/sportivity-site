TITLE: Somebody Said Your Name Last Week. Or They Didn't.
SUBTITLE: Most work is handed out in a thirty second conversation in a car park, and you are not in the car park. What I got wrong about qualifications, why weak ties beat close mates, and the twenty minutes a week that fixes it.

There is a conversation happening about you that you will never hear.

It happens in a car park after a meeting. Two people walking back to their cars, and one of them says, "we're going to need somebody for that Manchester piece." The other one thinks for a second. And a name arrives, or it doesn't.

That is the whole thing. Thirty seconds by a wing mirror is where most work actually gets handed out, and you were not in the car park.

## I believed the paper would do it

I did the master's. I did the Level 7 in leadership. The certificates are in a drawer somewhere and I am glad I did them, because they taught me things I still use every week.

They have never once won me a piece of work.

What they did was get me through a door I was already stood next to. Somebody had said my name first. The paper just meant nobody had to justify the decision afterwards.

It took me a long time to admit that, because I did not like what it said about the world. I wanted the best prepared person to win. I had been the best prepared person for years, sat in meetings with better notes than anybody else, watching the work go to a bloke who played golf with the right person.

I told myself he was lucky.

He was not lucky. He was known.

## The bit that makes it bearable

Here is where it turns, and this is the part that took me far too long to see.

A network is not who you know. If it was, this would be a piece about accidents of birth and there would be nothing in it for either of us.

A network is who can describe what you do.

Those are two completely different things. I know hundreds of people. Most of them could not tell you what I actually do for a living beyond "something with leadership". They cannot say my name in a car park, because they would not know which car park to say it in.

And the ones who can are not my closest mates. That surprised me.

There is a piece of research from 1973 by an American sociologist called Mark Granovetter, called "The Strength of Weak Ties". He asked people who had just changed jobs how they heard about the role. Of the ones who got there through a person, most had heard it from somebody they hardly ever saw. Not a best friend. The bloke from two jobs ago. The woman off the course.

Your close mates know everything you already know. They sit in the same rooms as you. The value is out on the edges, with people stood in car parks you will never visit.

## 3,800 jobs, all at once

On 10 September every Beefeater in the country shut. All 106 of them, with 89 Brewers Fayre sites going a few days before that. Around 3,800 jobs were put at risk in the same fortnight.

Some of those people will land somewhere in a month. Some will still be applying at Christmas.

And it will not sort itself out by who was best at the job. It will sort itself out by whose old area manager can still describe, in one line, what they were brilliant at.

That is not fair. It is just what happens.

## What to do on Monday

Two things. Neither of them is a networking event, because I would honestly rather clean the oven.

First, write the sentence. One line, plain English, saying what you are good at and who you are good for. Not your job title. "I sort out rotas in places where the staffing is a mess" beats "operations professional" every day of the week. If you cannot write that sentence, nobody can say it for you. That is the real problem, and you can fix it this afternoon.

Second, make a list of ten people who have watched you work. Not friends. People who saw you do the job. Old managers, a supplier, someone off a course, a client who moved on somewhere else.

Send one of them a message a week. Not asking for anything, because people can smell that a mile off and it wrecks the very thing you are building. Just tell them what you are up to at the minute, or ask them something specific about what they are doing. Ten weeks, ten people, then go round again.

That is twenty minutes a week. In a year you will have had a hundred small conversations, and your name will be sat in a hundred heads with a sentence attached to it.

## The honest bit

I am not telling you merit does not matter. It does. Being good is the price of getting in the door, and if the work is rubbish then none of this saves you.

But being good in private is a hobby.

Hard work beats talent when talent doesn't work hard. I still believe that, every word of it. I have just stopped pretending anybody notices hard work on its own.

So, who said your name last week?

And if you have no idea, who could have?
