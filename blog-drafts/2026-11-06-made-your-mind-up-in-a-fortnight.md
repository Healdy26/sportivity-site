A manager I was working with last year said something to me that I have not been able to put down since.

We were going through his team, name by name, the way you do. He got to one lad and said, "yeah, he's alright, he's not really one for the bigger stuff though."

So I asked him what made him say that.

Long pause. Then: "He just isn't. You can tell."

I pushed, gently, because that is the job. And after a bit of digging we got to the actual evidence. In his second week, nearly three years earlier, the lad had been asked a question in a meeting and he had gone bright red and said he did not know.

Three years. One blush. A permanent verdict.

## How four seconds becomes three years

Chris Argyris put a name to this back in 1970, and it is one of the most useful things I have ever learned. He called it the ladder of inference.

It works like this. There is everything that happened in that room, all of it, far too much for anyone to hold. You select a bit of it, usually without noticing which bit. You add a meaning to the bit you selected. You make an assumption off the back of that meaning. You reach a conclusion. And then the conclusion quietly becomes a belief, and you act on the belief.

That is six moves, and the whole thing takes about four seconds. You are never aware of doing any of it. By the time you get to the top you are not holding an interpretation any more. You are holding a fact. "He isn't one for the bigger stuff." Said with total confidence, because it feels like something you observed rather than something you built.

Here is the part that does the real damage though. Argyris spotted that it loops. Once the belief is up there, it decides what you select next time.

So you stop putting him in front of clients. Which means he never gets practice in front of clients. Which means the one time he does, he is rusty and nervous, and you think, there you go. Proof. Your own belief handed you the evidence.

The lad did nothing in those three years to earn that. He was just never given a room to be good in.

## Nobody is immune, and that includes me

I would love to tell you this is a thing other managers do.

I have done it. I have had people on my own teams who I had quietly filed under "sound, but keep him where he is", and I could not have told you why if you had made me. I just knew. And knowing without being able to say why is the exact feeling you should learn to distrust in yourself, because it is almost always a ladder you climbed years ago and forgot about.

I see the same shape everywhere I work, not just in businesses. Ofsted published its own commissioned research on 9 October, and nearly nine in ten schools said the inspection had harmed staff wellbeing. Set aside the row about the watchdog for a minute, because the bit that interests me is older and simpler. One visit, one verdict, and then months of living underneath it either side. We do that to institutions, and we do a quieter version of it to people in our own buildings, usually within a fortnight of them starting.

A judgement made in one moment is not wrong because the moment was fake. It is wrong because one moment is not enough data to build a career on.

## What to do on Monday

Pick the person you have the lowest expectations of. You will know who straight away, which is itself the problem.

Now write down the actual thing you saw. Not what it meant, not what it told you about them, just what happened. Where you were, what was said, roughly when.

Three things usually fall out of that.

Sometimes you cannot find a specific event at all, and the whole verdict turns out to be a feeling you have been topping up for years. Sometimes you find the event and it is genuinely small, like a blush in week two. And occasionally you find something real and recent and serious, in which case brilliant, you now have the thing you should have said to them months ago.

Any of those three is a better place to stand than "you can tell".

Then do the harder half. Give them one room to be good in. One job slightly above where you have been keeping them, with enough support that it is fair. Not a test you have privately already marked. A proper go.

Some of them will not take it. That is allowed. But you will have found that out from evidence instead of from a story you wrote in four seconds and then spent three years confirming.

The question I would leave you with is not about them. It is about you.

Who have you stopped expecting anything from, and when did you last check whether that was still true?
