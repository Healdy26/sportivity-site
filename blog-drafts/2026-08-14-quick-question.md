I watched it happen in an office in Bolton last month. A lad had his headphones on, head down, clearly deep in something. His manager wandered over, tapped him on the shoulder, and said the six most expensive words in business.

"Sorry, have you got a quick sec?"

The quick sec took about forty seconds. A yes, a nod, a where-are-we-with-that. Then the manager wandered off happy. The lad turned back to his screen, and I watched him just sit there. Reading the same line three times. Trying to remember where he'd been before the tap on the shoulder. It took him a good while to get going again.

That tap cost the business a lot more than forty seconds. And nobody in the room had the faintest idea.

## The bit nobody sees

There's a number doing the rounds this year that stopped me in my tracks. When someone gets interrupted at work, it takes an average of **twenty-three minutes** to get back to what they were doing properly. Not to look busy again. To actually be back in it.

And here's the kicker. The research reckons most knowledge workers now get interrupted every three minutes or so. Some studies put it at more than two hundred times a day once you count the pings, the tabs, the taps on the shoulder. Add it up and the average person is getting somewhere between two and three hours of genuine focus in a working day. The rest is spent either being interrupted, or climbing back out of the last interruption.

That's not laziness. That's not a bad team. That's the shape of the modern working day, and most leaders are making it worse without meaning to.

## Attention leaves a residue

There's a lovely bit of theory behind this from a researcher called Sophie Leroy. She called it **attention residue**. When you switch from one task to another, a chunk of your head stays stuck on the first one. You're in the meeting, but part of you is still finishing the email. You're back on the report, but part of you is still chewing on the quick question.

You never get all of yourself in the room. And the more you switch, the thinner you're spread, until you finish a nine-hour day genuinely knackered and not quite able to say what you actually did.

Sound familiar? It should. Because as leaders, we are often the single biggest source of other people's residue. Every "quick question", every "just flagging this", every "no rush but", lands like that tap on the shoulder. We think we're being responsive. We're actually being expensive.

## What good leaders do instead

You can't kill interruptions. You run a business, not a monastery. But you can stop treating focus like it's free and interruptions like they're weightless. Here's the one thing I'd have every leader try this week.

**Give your team a protected hour, and go first.** Pick a slot, say nine until ten, and make it understood that in that hour nobody taps anyone on the shoulder unless the building is on fire. No meetings booked into it. No "quick secs". You lead it by doing it yourself, visibly, headphones on, door shut, phone in the drawer.

Then batch your own quick questions. Instead of firing them off one at a time across the day like little grenades, keep a running list and hand them over in one go, once. You'll be amazed how many of them answer themselves before you get there.

That's it. One protected hour and one batched list. It costs nothing and it hands your people back the thing they need most to do good work, which is a stretch of time long enough to actually think.

## The honest question

Have a proper look at yesterday. How many times did you tap someone on the shoulder, physically or by message, because it was easier for you in that moment? And how many of those genuinely couldn't have waited an hour?

If you're anything like the manager in Bolton, the number will surprise you. He wasn't a bad boss. He cared about his team. He just had no idea what a quick question really costs, because the cost never shows up on his desk. It shows up on theirs.

Protect the deep work. It's where the real value in your business is actually made, and right now most of us are giving it away forty seconds at a time.
