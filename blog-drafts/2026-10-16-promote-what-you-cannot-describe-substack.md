TITLE: You Cannot Promote What You Cannot Describe
SUBTITLE: Thirty percent of UK workers think being in the office gets you promoted. The same thirty percent think delivering revenue does. That is not a working from home row. It is a much more uncomfortable problem, and it is the reason you cannot get out of your own business.

I have sat in a lot of promotion conversations over the years. Different rooms, different sectors, same script.

A name goes up on the whiteboard. Somebody nods. And then somebody says the line.

"He's always about, isn't he."

That is it. That is the evidence. The room relaxes, because a reason has been offered and nobody has to do any more thinking. The name stays on the board.

I used to think that was laziness. I do not think that any more. I think it is something worse, and quieter, and much more fixable.

## Thirty and thirty

There is research out from HiBob in September that put a number on it. They asked UK workers what actually gets you promoted.

Visibility to senior leadership came out at 34 percent. Constant availability, 33. Being in the office, 30.

Delivering revenue also came out at 30.

Read that again. In the heads of the people who work for you, turning up scores level with bringing in the money. And being available beats it.

Your feed will turn this into another row about working from home. It is not one. I have no interest in telling you where your people should sit, and neither has the research. HiBob's chief people officer, Nirit Peled-Muntz, put it better than I could: the law can give people the right to ask for flexibility, but it "cannot change what managers choose to reward."

What managers choose to reward. That is the whole thing.

## Why we count chairs

Here is the honest bit, and I include myself in it.

Presence is easy to measure. You can see it from your desk. It takes no preparation, no notes, no memory of what happened in June. It feels objective, because a person was either there or they were not.

Contribution is hard. To describe what somebody actually did, you have to have been paying attention for months, and you have to have written some of it down, and you have to be able to separate the work from the noise around the work.

So we drift to the easy number. We always do. I wrote a while back about Goodhart's Law, how a measure stops being any good the moment it becomes the target. This is the mirror image of it. When you have no measure at all, you do not become neutral. You fall back on the crudest visible one you have got.

And nobody has ever been pulled up for promoting the bloke who was always about.

## The bit that keeps you stuck

If you run the business, this costs you more than a bad promotion.

You cannot hand work to somebody whose strengths you cannot name. You just can't. Delegation is not a personality trait. It is knowing, specifically, what this person is better at than you are. If the best you have got is "she's reliable" and "he's been here ages", then every real decision comes back to your desk, because your desk is the only place where anyone knows anything.

That is not a delegation problem. That is a description problem wearing a delegation costume.

The owners I meet who are genuinely trapped in their own businesses are almost never short of good people. They are short of language about their good people.

## What to do on Monday

One page per person. You write it, not them.

Not a review form, not a competency grid, nothing that needs HR software. A page with their name at the top and one question underneath: what changed in this business because of them since June?

Give yourself twenty minutes a person. Write in sentences, not bullets. Name actual things. The client who stayed. The process that stopped falling over. The new lad who settled in because somebody sat with him on his first week.

Then notice how it feels. If you fill the page easily, good, you know your people and your next promotion will be a decent one. If you get four lines in and stall, you have just found out that you have been marking somebody on attendance for a year and calling it performance. That is worth knowing on a Monday in October rather than in a resignation meeting in March.

Do it quarterly. Twenty minutes a head, four times a year. For a team of six that is eight hours a year to always be able to answer the only question that matters about the people who work for you.

## KPIs should be Keep People Informed

I have said that for years and I will keep saying it, because the whole of this comes down to whether the people around you know what you actually value about them.

Most people who leave do not leave over money. They leave because nobody in the building could say, out loud and specifically, what they were good at. That is a lonely way to spend your Tuesdays.

So here is the question, and it is the same one whether you have got sixty staff or two.

If your best person walked in tomorrow morning and asked you what they were good at, could you answer for longer than ten seconds?

If the honest answer is no, that is not a character flaw. It is twenty minutes and a blank page. And if you want to talk it through with somebody, my diary is open.
