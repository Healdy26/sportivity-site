Somebody will ask you this month how your year's been.

You'll say busy. They'll nod, because they said it too, and neither of you will have said a single thing.

Then in January you'll sit down and write a plan for next year off the back of that. Off a feeling. A vague sense that it was alright, or hard, or better than last year. And you'll build twelve months on top of it.

By the time you finish this you'll have an honest account of your year on one page, and the one question to open January with. It takes an hour.

## What Drucker made people do

Peter Drucker wrote a piece for Harvard Business Review in 1999 called Managing Oneself, and buried in it is the most useful thing I have ever stolen from a management book.

He called it feedback analysis. Whenever you make a decision that matters, you write down what you expect to happen. Then nine or twelve months later you come back and compare what you expected with what actually happened. That's it. No model, no grid, no training course.

He said he'd been doing it for fifty years and it caught him out every time.

Here's why it works, and it isn't the writing down. It's the gap. Nobody can remember what they expected. We remember how things turned out, and then we quietly tell ourselves we saw it coming all along. So you lose the one bit of information that would have made you better next time, which is the size of the distance between your judgement and reality.

Ask most owners in December how their year went and you get a mood. Ask them what they thought would happen in March and they've got nothing.

Now, you didn't write anything down in January. I know. Neither did I, not properly.

But you've been keeping a record all year and you didn't realise.

## Your diary is the only honest thing in the business

Your accounts tell you what came in. Your diary tells you where you actually were.

And it doesn't flatter you. It can't. It was filled in by you, a day at a time, with no idea anyone would read it back. There is no business plan, no strategy day and no set of values in your building half as truthful as twelve months of your own calendar.

So that's the hour. Scroll back to January and go month by month. Three columns, on paper. I use a reMarkable pad, you can use the back of an envelope.

**One: what actually happened that month.** One line. The real event, not how it felt. "Lost the Oldham contract." "Took Sarah on." "Three weeks of tendering, won nothing."

**Two: what did I think would happen?** Only where you can honestly remember. Leave it blank the rest of the time, and resist the urge to fill blanks in from hindsight, because that's the whole trick and you'll be tempted every line.

**Three: what did I do because of it?**

That third column is the one that matters, and I'll tell you now what you'll find, because I find it in nearly every owner I sit down with. It's nearly empty.

Not a bad year. A year that went past and changed nothing. Things happened to you, you coped with all of them brilliantly, and then the next thing happened. Twelve months of coping reads exactly like twelve months of progress from the inside, and nothing like it on paper.

Two rules so it actually gets done. Give it an hour, not a day, because a day means never. And do it on your own, not in a management meeting, because the second somebody else is in the room you start performing instead of remembering.

## The question to open January with

Last month I gave you an hour a fortnight with the manager you're trying to grow. Same slot, never moved. So here is what to do in the first one.

Don't ask them for their goals. Everybody has a goal in January and nobody has one by March.

Ask them this instead.

**"What did we do this year that you'd want us to do again?"**

It works because it's a question they can actually answer. Goals are invented on the spot to please you. This one has a real answer sat in their memory already, and the answer tells you what they value, which is worth more to you than any objective they'll write down.

Be ready for it to be small. In my experience it's almost never the thing you'd have guessed, and it's rarely the big win. It's the week you covered for them when their mum was ill. The time you let them run the Thursday meeting. The one honest conversation in the van on the way back.

Then, once they've answered, ask the other half. **"And what did we do that you'd want us to stop?"**

Write down what they say, word for word, in their words not yours. Don't fix it in the room. You'll want to, badly, because you'll know the answer in about four seconds. That was last month's issue and the rule hasn't changed.

## One AI trick worth stealing

The hour with the diary is better done by hand. But there's a version worth running afterwards, because your own eyes will skip straight past the patterns you're in the middle of.

Export your calendar for the last twelve months. Strip the client names out if they're in the meeting titles, that's somebody else's business and it doesn't belong in a chat window. Then paste the titles into Claude or ChatGPT with this:

```
You are a blunt non-executive director. Below is my calendar for
the last twelve months, meeting titles only.

[paste]

Tell me:
1. What this business actually sold this year, judging only by
   where my time went.
2. The three biggest gaps between what I say my priorities are
   (list yours here) and where the hours actually went.
3. Anything recurring in my diary that has stopped earning its
   place.

Quote dates back at me so I can check you. Plain English,
British spelling. Do not be encouraging.
```

That last line is doing more work than the rest of the prompt. Leave it off and it'll hand you a lovely summary of what a strong year you've had, because that's what these things are built to do.

The first answer is usually the one that stings. What you sold this year and what you think you sell are often two different businesses.

## What's caught my eye

**Your managers like the job. They're leaving anyway.** Ciphr surveyed 500 UK managers and published on 1 October. A quarter regularly think about quitting because of the pressure of managing people, and 31% expect to be gone inside twelve months. Then the number that stopped me: 85% say they like being a people manager. And 31% say their workload is unmanageable most or all of the time. Claire Hawes, Chief People and Operations Officer at Ciphr, puts it plainly, that managers are being asked to do more with less budget and less resource. ([Fair Play Talks has the write-up.](https://www.fairplaytalks.com/2026/10/01/one-in-four-uk-managers-regularly-think-about-quitting-as-people-pressures-mount/))

They haven't gone off the work. They've gone off a version of the job that has nothing in it but the job. Go and look at your manager's year in your own diary, in that third column. If there isn't a single hour in twelve months that was about them rather than about the work, you've just been handed the reason, and you've got a year to do something with it.

**The thing nobody cancels on purpose.** The Youth Sport Trust's PE and School Sport Annual Report 2026, launched in Parliament in June, found PE is the most frequently cancelled subject in schools. Only 57% of teachers say their school manages the recommended two hours a week. Curriculum time given to PE has dropped from 8.6% to 7.3% since London 2012. ([Youth Sport Trust on the squeeze.](https://www.youthsporttrust.org/news-listings/news/new-data-shows-pe-hours-continue-to-be-squeezed-in-schools))

Nobody sat in a meeting and voted to cut PE. There was never a decision. It was just always the thing that moved when something more urgent turned up, and it moved so many times that the timetable changed shape without anyone choosing it.

That's how everything worth doing dies. Not in a decision. In a hundred small reschedulings, every one of them perfectly reasonable on the day. Which is exactly what will happen to your hour a fortnight in February if you let it.
