It's dark by four now, so let's talk about why you're still the last one in the building.

Last month I said something I meant. If you are taking somebody young on, spend the money on the supervisor, not the apprentice. Teach the supervisor first.

Which leaves a question hanging. Teach them what?

Here's where I'd start, because it's the thing I see eating more owners than anything else in November. Every problem in the business still ends up on your desk. Not the big strategic ones. The ordinary ones. The supplier who's messed the order up. The two lads who aren't speaking. The customer who wants a favour.

You promoted somebody eighteen months ago specifically so that stuff would stop reaching you. It reaches you anyway. And you've probably decided, quietly, that they're just not quite ready yet.

They might be ready. You might be the reason it keeps coming back.

## The triangle nobody draws on a whiteboard

Stephen Karpman worked this out in 1968, and it comes out of transactional analysis, which is the lens I look at nearly everything through. He called it the drama triangle. Three seats, and a business can spin round them for years.

Somebody arrives stuck. That's the first seat. Not weak, not lazy, just genuinely stuck and holding a problem they can't see through. So they bring it to you.

And you sit down in the second seat, because you're good and you're quick and you care. You solve it. Four minutes, maybe six. They go away relieved, you feel useful, and something small gets taught in that room that neither of you notices: problems get solved upstairs.

Then comes the third seat, and this is the one that catches people out. Six weeks later you're stood in your kitchen at half nine at night, fuming, saying out loud that nobody in that place takes any responsibility. So you go hard on them. Maybe in a meeting, maybe in an email at eleven at night with a full stop after every sentence. They go quiet. You feel like a swine about it. And when they next get stuck, they bring it to you even faster, because now there's a bit of fear in it too.

Round it goes.

Here's my stance on it, and it's not the comfortable one. The rescuer keeps the triangle spinning. Not the stuck person. You. Every rescue is a small, kind, well-meant lesson that they can't do it without you, and you have been teaching it beautifully for eighteen months.

## The swap

Getting off the triangle is boring, which is why almost nobody does it. It's a conversation, and it takes longer the first few times than just solving the thing.

Next time one of your managers arrives with a problem, don't answer it. Ask three questions and stop.

**"What have you already tried?"** Half the time they've tried something sensible and just want a witness. That's a two minute conversation, not a rescue.

**"What do you think we should do?"** They will nearly always have an answer. It comes out hedged, phrased as a question, wrapped in "but you probably want to..." because they're used to you having the real answer in your pocket. Let them finish it.

**"What do you need from me to do it?"** This is the one that changes the room, because it hands the thing back without dropping it. Sometimes they need a budget, or your name on an email, or permission to say no to a customer. Give them that. Don't give them the answer.

Then comes the hard bit, and I'm not going to dress it up. Their plan will be about eighty per cent as good as yours.

Take it anyway.

Yours is a better plan that teaches nobody anything and comes straight back to you next month. Theirs is a slightly worse plan that makes them a leader by March. I've watched owners lose good managers over that twenty per cent. The manager doesn't leave because they got told off. They leave because nothing was ever really theirs.

One caveat, because it's not a rule for everything. Some things genuinely are yours. Anything to do with safety, money out the door, or somebody's job is your call and you should take it. The triangle isn't about abandoning people. It's about knowing which seat you're sat in.

## What to actually give them, now the course has gone

There's a practical reason this matters more than it did last year.

From 1 September, sixteen apprenticeship standards stopped being funded for new starts. Among them Team Leader Level 3, Operations Manager Level 5, Coaching Professional Level 5 and Chartered Manager Level 6. Team Leader was the most used apprenticeship in the country to lose its funding, 12,670 starts last year, and only eighty of those were under nineteen. ([FE Week has the full list](https://feweek.co.uk/apprenticeships-purge-team-leader-and-chartered-manager-among-16-axed-standards/), 15 March 2026.)

I sell leadership development for a living, so you'd expect me to call that a disaster. I'm not going to. Most of the managers I meet never got near a funded course anyway, and the ones who did came back with a folder.

But it does mean that if you were waiting for a course to develop your supervisor, the waiting is over. It's you now.

So here's the version that costs nothing. An hour a fortnight in the diary, same slot, never cancelled. One real decision that is genuinely theirs, with a budget attached and your name nowhere near it. And one question at the end of every hard week: what would you do differently?

That's it. That's the whole programme. An hour a fortnight for a year is twenty six hours of somebody senior giving a new leader proper attention, which is more than any Level 3 was ever going to give them.

The only thing that makes it work is that you don't cancel it. The first time you move it for a client meeting, they learn exactly where they sit in the order of things.

## One AI trick worth stealing

You cannot hand back what you haven't noticed you're carrying. So start there.

Jot down everything you personally dealt with last week. Messy list, back of an envelope, whatever. Then paste it into Claude or ChatGPT with this:

```
You are a blunt operations director. I run a [size] business and I
am the bottleneck.

Below is everything I personally handled last week.
[paste your list]

Sort every single item into three piles:
1. Mine. Only I can genuinely decide this.
2. Theirs. I did it out of habit or because it was quicker.
3. Theirs, but they are not ready. Say precisely what they would
   need from me to be ready.

Then write me the actual words to hand back the three biggest
items in pile 2. Plain English, warm, direct, British spelling.
Do not soften it into a vague request I would never say out loud.
```

Two warnings from doing this myself.

Pile two will be much bigger than you're expecting, and your first instinct will be to argue with it. Sit with that feeling for a minute, it's telling you something.

And hand back three things, not thirty. Dumping half your week on somebody on a Monday morning isn't delegation, it's a different kind of drama entirely.

## What's caught my eye

**Nobody's telling their manager they're struggling.** Mental Health UK's Burnout Report 2026 (published 16 January) found that 35% of workers wouldn't feel comfortable telling a manager they were under high or extreme stress, up three points on the year before. For 18 to 24 year olds it's 39%. And 29% said their employer talks about mental health, but their managers haven't got the time, training or resources to do anything meaningful about it. ([The report is here.](https://mentalhealth-uk.org/news-and-insights/burnout-report-2026-high-stress-pushing-workers-into-sick-leave-as-just-one-in-four-feel-mental-health-is-genuinely-prioritised-and-supported-in-the-workplace/))

That last number is the one I keep coming back to. It isn't that managers don't care. It's that we've filled their week with everything they never handed over, and then wondered why there's no room left for the human bit. Which is the whole of this issue, really.

**More of us are moving, but not all of us.** Sport England's Active Lives figures (published 23 April) had 64.6% of adults hitting the activity guidelines, 30.9 million people, and 859,000 more than the year before. Genuinely good news. Then look underneath it: 73.2% of the most affluent adults are active against 53.8% of the least affluent. Nineteen points. ([Sport England's write-up.](https://www.sportengland.org/news-and-inspiration/england-getting-more-active-not-everyone-benefiting))

A national number going up while the gap holds steady isn't a win, it's an average hiding two different countries. If you run anything that gets people moving, that gap is your actual job.
