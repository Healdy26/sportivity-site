## Note 2: busyness (link Issue 1)

Busyness is a disease.

I ran a business, played semi-pro football, had three kids and money in the bank. On paper I'd made it.

In reality I was trying to please everybody, and it broke me.

Activity isn't progress. A full diary isn't a full life.

These days I run five roles on about four hours of proper focus a day. The rest is noise I've learned to leave alone.

How I do it is in the first issue of The Monthly Edge.

## Note 3: the number everyone hits (link Goodhart post)

When a measure becomes a target, it stops being a good measure.

Goodhart said that in 1975, and every business I walk into is still proving him right.

The sales team hits the call number, and the calls get worse. The school hits the attendance figure, and the kids are there in body only.

The number gets hit. Nobody trusts it. Everybody knows.

I hate KPIs. They should be Keep People Informed.

## Note 4: kind or tough (link Issue 3)

Kind or tough. Pick one.

That's the choice most leaders think they're making, and it's the wrong question.

This summer I watched three leaders get tested in public in the same month. Different worlds. Same test. Could they hold kindness and toughness at the same time, in the same room, without dropping either?

The ones who could, you'll remember.

The ones who couldn't, you already have.

## Note 5: peaks and troughs (link Issue 2)

The phone doesn't ring for a fortnight and you decide it's over.

Then it rings six times in a day and you decide you're a genius.

Neither is true. It's the same business on a different Tuesday.

Eight years on my own and I still catch myself doing it. The trick isn't to flatten the peaks and the troughs. It's to stop reading them as a verdict on you.

## Note 6: nobody tells the boss the truth (link Issue 4)

The feedback doesn't stop when you become the boss.

It just stops being true.

Think about the last time someone who works for you said "that was the wrong call and here's why". If you can't remember it, that isn't because you've stopped making wrong calls.

It's because you've got the one job where honesty costs the person giving it something.

## Note 7: the 82% (link Issue 4)

82% of managers were never trained to lead anyone.

Promoted on the Friday because they were good at the job. Expected to be a leader on the Monday.

Then we hand them a 22 year old fresh out of college and act surprised when it doesn't go well.

And we blame the 22 year old.

If you've promoted someone this year and their development so far is a congratulations email and a new job title, you already know which number is your problem.

## Note 8: the AI trick (link Issue 4)

I use AI as the person in the room who'll disagree with me.

Not to write the thing. To attack the thing before somebody else does.

"You're a sceptical MD with no stake in my feelings. Here's the decision. Give me the three strongest arguments against it and the assumption I haven't questioned. Be blunt."

Do it before you've decided, not after.

And when one of the answers makes your stomach drop a bit, that's the one.

The full prompt is in this month's issue:

## Note 9: no link, just the thing you believe

Vulnerability is a superpower.

I know how that sounds. I used to think it sounded soft too, right up until pretending I was fine nearly cost me everything.

The strongest leaders I've worked with aren't the ones who never wobble. They're the ones who can say "I don't know" in a room full of people who expect them to know.

Who in your business is allowed to say that out loud?

Take two minutes. Feel your feet on the floor. You are here.

## Note 10: what is this number making us do? (link Goodhart post)

Green numbers on a big screen. Everyone in the room pleased with themselves.

Attendance up. Retention up. The chief exec said the coaching had clearly improved.

I'd been watching those sessions for months. The coaching hadn't improved. It had got easier.

The coaches had worked out that the way to keep the numbers green was to keep every child happy and never stretch anyone. So they didn't.

The graph climbed. The coaching slid. And nobody in that room could see it, because the number said otherwise.

One question would have caught it. What is this number making us do?

## Note 11: adult to adult (no link, grounding close)

Psychological safety has been turned into a poster in the corridor and a line in the induction pack.

It isn't comfort. It's being safe enough to be challenged.

Sit in most meetings and somebody is playing the parent and somebody is playing the child. The rank does the talking. And the actual problem never gets said out loud.

Adult to adult is the whole job. Same level, no rank in the room, for ten minutes.

Try it once this week and watch what comes out that never came out before.

Take two minutes. Feel your feet on the floor. You are here.

## Note 12: count to five (link Issue 4)

Ask the question. Then shut up and count to five.

Most leaders fill the silence in about two seconds, answer their own question, and then wonder why nobody ever tells them anything.

The polite answer comes first. The honest one comes after it, and only if you leave a gap big enough for it to fit in.

Five seconds feels like a fortnight when you're the one sat there saying nothing. Do it anyway.

That's one of three things that actually work. The other two are in this month's Monthly Edge.

## Note 13: the next first step (no link)

Nobody fails because the plan was too small.

They fail because the plan was so big they never started it.

I see it every week. An owner who knows exactly what needs to change, has known for a year, and is waiting for the quiet fortnight that never turns up.

It's not about achieving everything. It's about taking the next first step.

One conversation you've been avoiding. One meeting cancelled that nobody wanted anyway. One thing off the list.

What's yours, and could you do it before Friday?

## Note 14: the list of things you've stopped (link Issue 1)

Every business I walk into has a list of the things it's starting.

Hardly any of them have a list of the things they've stopped.

That's the problem right there. We keep adding. New system, new meeting, new report nobody reads. Then we wonder why everyone's knackered and nothing has actually moved.

Progress isn't what you add. Most of the time it's what you're brave enough to drop.

And the hardest ones to drop are the things you're good at, because they feel like proof you're useful.

Write down one thing you'll stop this month. The cure I use is in the first issue.
