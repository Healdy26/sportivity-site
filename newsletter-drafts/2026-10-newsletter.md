Most grants are a faff for the money you get back.

This one isn't.

Since August, the government will fully fund apprenticeship training for smaller businesses taking on anyone aged 16 to 24. Stack the hiring grants on top and you are looking at up to eight thousand pounds towards a hire you were probably already thinking about.

That is not a rounding error in a twelve person business. That is a person.

**By the time you finish this, you will know exactly which pot you qualify for, and you will have a written ninety day plan for the young person before they walk through the door.** About twenty minutes, most of it clicking.

## Why this matters now

October is when the last piece lands, so this is the first month the full stack is claimable.

And there are over a million young people in this country not in education, employment or training. Roughly one in eight. Some of them are within ten miles of your office right now, and a decent number of them are perfectly good and just have not had anyone say yes to them yet.

I am not asking you to hire out of charity. I am telling you the maths has changed and most owners have not noticed.

## The map

> The 5 steps:
> 1. Work out which pot you actually qualify for
> 2. Check the apprenticeship training funding
> 3. Name the person who owns the first ninety days
> 4. Build the ninety day plan (AI does this in two minutes)
> 5. Write the advert that gets you a decent one

Steps 1, 2 and 4 are mechanics. Anyone can do them.

Steps 3 and 5 are the actual skill, and they are the reason most of these hires work or don't. Do not skim them.

## Step 1: Work out which pot you qualify for

**What to do.** There are several schemes, they have different rules, and the £8,000 figure in the press is a stack of them, not one cheque. Start with the Youth Jobs Grant because it is the biggest single lump.

Go to the Youth Jobs Grant page and read the eligibility box:

```
https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/youth-jobs-grant
```

The short version: **£3,000** for taking on an 18 to 24 year old who has been on Universal Credit and looking for work for six months or more. It is paid in two goes, £1,800 in month two and £1,200 in month five. It has been running since 30 June. The initial rollout covered 18 to 21 year olds and widens to all 18 to 24 year olds from autumn.

The rest of the stack, if you are taking on an apprentice:
- **£2,000** hiring grant for smaller employers taking on a 16 to 24 year old, live this month
- **£2,000** foundation apprenticeship incentive
- **£1,000** for younger apprentices, and for care leavers

Plus no employer National Insurance on an under 21, or on an apprentice under 25 earning below £50,270.

**What success looks like.** You have written down one number: what your specific hire is actually worth to you. Not the press release number. Yours.

## Step 2: Check the apprenticeship training funding

**What to do.** This is the change most people have missed, and it is the quiet big one.

Fully funded apprenticeship training for non-levy employers, that is you if your pay bill is under £3 million, used to stop at 21. It now covers **all eligible 16 to 24 year olds**, from August.

If you looked at an apprentice a year ago and the training cost put you off, that objection has gone.

Start here for the employer route and the Jobcentre Plus connection:

```
https://www.business.gov.uk/campaign/recruit-with-jobcentreplus/build-your-future-workforce/
```

**What success looks like.** You know whether you are going down the apprenticeship route or a straight hire, because they pull different money.

## Step 3: Name the person who owns the first ninety days

Mechanics over. This is where it is won or lost.

**What to do.** Before you advertise anything, pick one person in your business who is responsible for this young person's first ninety days. Write their name down.

One name. Not "the team".

A team cannot notice that a nervous nineteen year old has stopped asking questions. A person can.

Then put a recurring one hour slot in that person's diary with the young person's name on it, starting the week they arrive. Do it now, before the hire exists, because a slot booked in advance survives and a good intention does not.

**What success looks like.** There is a named human and a repeating calendar entry, and neither of them is you telling yourself you will keep an eye on it.

If your best supervisor is too busy for one hour a week, that is not a scheduling problem. That is your answer on capacity, and you have got it for free before it cost you a person.

## Step 4: Build the ninety day plan

**What to do.** Most young people are not failing your standard. They are guessing at it. And guessing looks exactly like not caring from where you are stood.

So write the standard down. Thirty, sixty, ninety days. Three lines each.

You do not need to draft this from scratch. Paste this in and edit what comes back:

```
You are an experienced operations manager in a UK SME.

Business: [what you do, how many staff]
The role: [job title, what they'll actually be doing day to day]
The person: [age, experience level, e.g. "19, first proper job,
no industry experience"]
Who's supervising them: [job title, and be honest about how much
time they realistically have]

Write me a 30/60/90 day plan for this hire.

For each stage give me:
1. Three things they should be able to do unsupervised by then
2. One thing that will probably go wrong at this stage, and the
   early warning sign
3. The three questions the supervisor should ask in the weekly 1:1

Keep it to one page. Plain English, no HR jargon. Assume the
supervisor has never developed anyone before.
```

**What success looks like.** One page you could hand to the supervisor and the young person on the same morning, and both would understand it.

## Step 5: Write the advert that gets you a decent one

**What to do.** The reason you get twelve poor applications is usually the advert. Most SME job ads for young roles read like a warning notice. "Must be able to work under pressure. Must be a self starter."

A nineteen year old reads that and hears "you will be on your own here".

The good ones apply where somebody promises to teach them something. Say that bit out loud:

```
Rewrite this job advert to appeal to a 16-24 year old with little
or no work experience who is good but lacks confidence.

[paste your current advert]

Rules:
- Lead with what they'll learn and who will teach them
- Name the person who'll be supporting them
- Cut every requirement that isn't genuinely essential
- Plain English, no corporate filler, British spelling
- Be honest about the hard parts, don't oversell it
- Under 300 words
```

**What success looks like.** You would have applied for it at nineteen.

## The pro bit

Here is the thing the steps above cannot give you, and it is the reason I wrote this issue.

**The money does not fix the thing that went wrong last time.**

I banged on last month about the 82 per cent. Managers promoted for being good at the job, never trained to lead anybody. Only 6 per cent of them reckon young recruits are ready for work.

Now read that next to a grant scheme.

We take our best doer, promote her, teach her nothing, hand her a teenager, and act surprised when it does not go well. A subsidy does not touch any of that. A subsidy just makes it cheaper to do again.

I have watched businesses take the money, run the same playbook, lose the young person by week eight, and quietly conclude that young people are hard work. The grant did not cause that. But it did pay for it.

So if you are going to spend money anywhere in this, spend it on the supervisor, not the apprentice. The apprentice's training is already funded. Nobody is funding the fact that the person receiving them has never been shown how to bring somebody on.

Teach the supervisor first. Everything else follows.
