Somebody offered to build it for nothing. That was the moment to slow down, not speed up.

This one catches good people with good intentions on both sides, which is exactly why it keeps happening.

You have worked out a way of doing something. It came out of years of being in the room and getting it wrong, so you know where the real snags are and nobody else does. Then someone with a platform and a team of developers says "I could knock that up for you in a month. No charge, happy to help."

And they mean it. That is what makes it hard.

Here is the bit that gets missed. Free does not mean cheap. Free means they own it. Your knowledge, which is the valuable part, walks straight into their product and becomes a feature with their name above it. You end up a grateful customer of your own idea.

So the question is not whether to say yes. Often you should, because the problem is real and your winter is not infinite.

The question is what gets written down before anybody sees the detail.

Three lines will do. What is being built, and to whose spec. Who is named as the partner it was designed with. And what happens to your share if it gets sold to somebody else.

Fifteen minutes with that agreed is worth more than any feature you could add afterwards.

Do it before the demo, not after. Once they have seen how the thing works, the knowledge has already moved and you have nothing left to trade with. That is why the version sat on my laptop is staying on my laptop until the frame is agreed, and it is costing me nothing to wait.

The instinct that makes this hard is a decent one. It feels mean to ask someone you like to sign something.

But paperwork is not distrust. It is what lets you stay friends when it does well.

What have you handed over for free this year because asking felt awkward?
