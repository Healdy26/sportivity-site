A manager I worked with was going through his team name by name. He got to one lad and said, "he's alright, he's not really one for the bigger stuff though."

I asked what made him say that.

Long pause. Then: "He just isn't. You can tell."

We dug. The actual evidence was this. In his second week, nearly three years earlier, the lad had been asked something in a meeting, gone bright red, and said he didn't know.

Three years. One blush. A permanent verdict.

Chris Argyris named this back in 1970. The ladder of inference. You select a scrap of what happened, add a meaning to it, assume, conclude, and the conclusion hardens into a belief. Six moves, about four seconds, and you never notice you climbed.

Then it loops, which is the bit that does the damage.

You stop putting him in front of clients. So he never gets practice in front of clients. So the one time he does, he's rusty, and you think: there you go, proof. Your own belief handed you the evidence.

He did nothing to earn that. He was just never given a room to be good in.

I've done it too, by the way. Filed people under "keep him where he is" and couldn't have told you why.

That feeling, knowing without being able to say why, is the one to distrust in yourself.

So pick whoever you expect least from. You'll know instantly, which is the problem. Write down the actual thing you saw. Not what it meant. What happened.

Then give them one room to be good in.

Who have you stopped expecting anything from, and when did you last check whether that was still true?
